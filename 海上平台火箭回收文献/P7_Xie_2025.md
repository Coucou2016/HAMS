## Abstract

The impact force effect on launch platform motion response represents a critical safety consideration that requires thorough  investigation  prior  to  sea-launch  implementation.  This  paper  examines  a  self-designed  semi-submersible launch platform comprising a box-shaped deck, six columns, and two pontoons, with sufficient structural stiffness to be analyzed as a rigid body. A proprietary code based on three-dimensional linear potential theory was developed for hydrodynamic  analysis  of the  launching  process.  The  Cummins  equation  was  implemented  to  calculate  platform responses  under  substantial  impact  force.  The  numerical  results  were  validated  through  comparison  with ANSYS/Aqwa commercial software for platform motion response in both launch and non-launch cases. Additionally, two model tests were conducted in a sea-keeping wave basin at a scale ratio of λ =1:40. The numerical results demonstrated good agreement with experimental data. Both numerical and experimental findings indicate that platform motion responses result from wave-induced effects and impact force/rocket weight effects, with the latter typically predominant. Numerical simulations revealed that in moderate sea states, maximum heave and pitch motions measure 0.6 m and 1°, respectively, suggesting the viability of sea-launch operations using the designed platform under these conditions.

Key words: sea-launch platform, impact force, wave basin experiments, numerical simulations

## 1 Introduction

Sea-based  launch  offers  several  advantages  over  landbased  alternatives,  including  mobility  and  flexibility  in launch  site  selection,  simplified  rocket  transportation,  and reduced risk of surrounding area damage from rocket debris, thereby garnering increasing industry attention (Song et al., 2021).  Sea-based  launches  encompass  two  categories:  cold launch, requiring secondary ignition after deck ejection, and thermal  launch,  utilizing  single  ignition  with  benefits  of simplified  launch  systems  and  enhanced  payload  capacity per  unit  space  (Konyukhov,  2004).  However,  thermal launch implementation presents challenges due to significant rocket wake impact force on the platform, potentially causing equilibrium position deviations and substantial translational and angular displacements (Wang et al., 2022). These deviations may adversely affect spacecraft attitude control during takeoff  and  significantly  influence  platform  motion response (Mu et al., 2023). Therefore, quantitative analysis of platform motion under impact force is essential for determining appropriate operational conditions to ensure launch success and platform safety.

Potential  theory  and  its  derivative  numerical  methods are commonly employed for simulating and predicting offshore platform hydrodynamic response. Zhang et al. (2020) investigated second-order hydrodynamic effects on floating wind  turbine  platforms,  comparing  frequency  and  time domain analyses across three platform configurations. Pham and  Shin  (2020)  integrated  potential  method  with  NREL FAST for fully coupled wind and wave simulations, achieving results consistent with model experiments. Zou et al. (2022, 2024) and Chen et al. (2024) employed an efficient constant parameter time-domain model (CPTDM) to examine multibody  system  nonlinear  dynamics,  demonstrating  superior accuracy  and  efficiency  particularly  at  resonant  frequency compared to alternative time-domain simulations. However, complex  phenomena  such  as  green  water and  slamming  in severe  sea  states  necessitate  model  experiments  or  CFD simulations.  Rivera-Arreba  et  al.  (2019)  utilized  secondorder potential-flow based solver and fully nonlinear Navier-Stokes equations solver to analyze semi-submersible floating platform  response  in  non-steep  waves,  revealing  potential flow  model  limitations  in  accurately  capturing  heave response and viscous effect relationships at natural frequency excitation.  Paulsen  et  al.  (2019)  conducted  physical  model tests  examining  wave  slamming  effects  on  monopile  wind turbine  foundations,  establishing  probability  formulas  for wave slamming and associated force. Zhu et al. (2022) and Huo et al. (2023) performed model tests and CFD simulations investigating floating wind turbine slamming characteristics under freak and irregular waves respectively.

Unlike floating  wind  turbines  or  offshore  drilling   platforms,  offshore  launch  platforms  are  subject  to  substantial instantaneous impact  loads  during  rocket  launches.   Understanding the platform’s motion response under these impact loads  is  essential  for  the  overall  layout  design  and  critical for  ensuring  safe  and  stable  operations.  When  a  rocket launches  from  an  offshore  platform,  the  platform  deviates from  its  equilibrium  position  and  experiences  significant translational and angular displacements. These displacements affect  both  spacecraft  attitude  control  during  takeoff (Cui and Hu, 2007) and the platform’s motion response. Consequently,  quantitative  analysis  of  the  platform’s  motion behavior under impact forces is necessary to determine suitable  operational  conditions  for  rocket  launches  and  ensure platform safety.  Several  studies  have  examined  the   hydrodynamic response characteristics of floating platforms under impact  loads  from  spacecraft  launches.  Wang  et  al.  (2003) investigated  ship  motion  characteristics  in  waves  under impact force through model tests, validating their established Kane’s dynamic model (Wang et al., 2001). Yu et al. (2021) analyzed  the  hydrodynamic  response  of  offshore  launch platforms  under  various  conditions  using  ANSYS/Aqwa three-dimensional potential commercial software, examining how  factors  such  as  draft,  impact  load,  and  launch  phase influence  platform  motion  response.  Pang  et  al.  (2023) developed  a  test  method  for  evaluating  impact  vibration acceleration  response  of offshore  rocket  launch  platforms studying  launch  platform  impact  response  during  cold launch through finite element analysis and shipboard experiments.  Their  findings  revealed  rapid  acceleration  peak decay  during  ejection,  followed  by  oscillating  acceleration signals  during  ignition.  Hou  (2024)  employed  STAR-CCM+  to  examine  motion  responses  of  various  offshore launch  platform  types  under  different  wave  conditions, launch positions, and platform motion phases.

Currently,  limited  research  exists  regarding  spacecraft launch  platform  motion  responses  under  impact  loads  in complex  marine  environments.  Engineering  practice requires a method for rapid prediction of launching platform hydrodynamic responses to impact loads in waves.

This  paper  introduces  a  self-designed  offshore  launch platform and validates its launch mission feasibility through numerical  and  experimental  methods.  Furthermore,  an  inhouse code based on linear three-dimensional potential theory is  developed  to  calculate  the  platform’s  hydrodynamic response,  accounting  for  both  wave  excitation  and  launch impact  force  effects.  The  validity  and  accuracy  of the  inhouse code are verified through comparisons with ANSYS Aqwa commercial software and model test results, demonstrating  the  method’s  applicability  for  relevant  engineering applications.

This paper is arranged as follows. In Section 2, the designed launch platform is presented. In Section 3, the mode test  and  numerical  method  are  presented  respectively,  and the in-house code is verified. In Section 4, the hydrodynamic calculations of the launch platform under different sea and working  conditions  are  carried  out,  and  the  hydrodynamic characteristics of the  launch  platform  are  analyzed  in   conjunction with the results of the model tests. Finally, conclusions and prospects for future work are given in Section 5.

## 3.1.2 Experiment set-up

Based on the wave basin dimensions and wave generator capacity, a scale ratio of λ=1:40 was selected for the platform model  depicted  in  Fig.  3.  The  model’s  main  structure  was constructed from fiber reinforced plastics (FRP) and maintained  the  identical  configuration  shown  in  Fig.  1.  During testing,  the  platform  was  positioned  at  the  basin’s  center, with  a  dynamic  positioning  system  restricting  surge,  sway, and  yaw  motions  within  acceptable  limits  (Sun  and  Yin, 2023; Hu et al., 2022). The platform’s superstructure geometry closely resembled that of the prototype. Prior to testing, the model’s weight, center of gravity, and mass moments of inertia  were  measured  and  calibrated  to  correspond  with prototype specifications. The experiment maintained identical  Froude  and  Strouhal  numbers  between  the  model  and prototype, ensuring gravitational and inertial force similarities.  The  rocket  was  positioned  vertically  on  the  platform deck  before  launch  and  subsequently  propelled  skyward using  a  small  solid  rocket  motor,  while  the  platform’s motion response  was  recorded  throughout  the  entire   process.

## 3.2  Numerical method and validation

## 3.2.1 Numerical method

This  study  employs  time  domain  dynamic  simulations based on linear three-dimensional potential theory to investigate  platform  motions  under  substantial  impact  forces. Both  commercial  software  and  a  self-developed  code  were utilized to perform numerical simulations and compare with model test results. The global earth fixed coordinate system $( O _ { 0 } { - } X _ { 0 } Y _ { 0 } Z _ { 0 } )$ originates  at  the  mean  free  surface,  while  the local  body  fixed  coordinate  system  (O-XYZ)  originates  at the projection of the center of gravity on the mean free surface, as defined in Fig. 4. The wave direction is designated as 0° when traveling along the X-axis and $9 0 ^ { \circ }$ when traveling along the Y-axis. The wetted surface edges of the numerical model differ slightly from the experimental model for simplification  purposes,  which  is  not  expected  to  significantly affect platform responses. All simulations maintain zero forward speed. The numerical simulations utilize the prototype launch platform parameters.

![](P7_Xie_2025_images/47829731eeaf066f084f67e782ca706942671a0c108c5be033bc23f69d867b42.jpg)  
Fig. 4.   Wet surface of launch platform.

The  equations  of motion  were  established  in  the  selfdeveloped in-house code for the following three cases:

(1) Regular wave without impact force

The  equation  of  motion  in  the  time  domain  can  be derived directly by converting the equation in the frequency domain for regular waves:

$$
\begin{array}{c} \left[ \boldsymbol {m} + \boldsymbol {A} (\omega) \right] \ddot {\boldsymbol {X}} (t) + \left[ \boldsymbol {b} + \boldsymbol {B} (\omega) \right] \dot {\boldsymbol {X}} (t) + \boldsymbol {K X} (t) = \\ \boldsymbol {F} _ {\mathrm{h}} (t) + \boldsymbol {F} _ {\mathrm{d}} (t) + \boldsymbol {F} _ {\mathrm{g}} (t), \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

m bwhere    represents  the  structural  mass  matrix,    denotes Kthe artificial linear damping matrix,   indicates the hydrostatic  stiffness  matrix, $F _ { \mathrm { h } } ( t )$ represents  the  hydrodynamic wave  incident  force, $F _ { \mathrm { d } } ( t )$ denotes  the  wave  diffraction force, and $F _ { \mathrm { g } } ( t )$ A(ω) represents the gravitational force.   and

B(ω)  represent  the  frequency-dependent  added  mass  and damping  coefficient,  respectively.  ω  represents  the  angular frequency of the wave, and t denotes time.

(2) Irregular wave without impact force

The motion of the in-house code in irregular waves follows  Cummins  equation  (Pang  et  al.,  2023)  and  which  is expressed in a convolution integral form:

$$
\left(\boldsymbol {m} + \boldsymbol {A} _ {\infty}\right) \ddot {\boldsymbol {X}} (t) + \boldsymbol {b} \dot {\boldsymbol {X}} (t) + \boldsymbol {K} \boldsymbol {X} (t) + \int_ {0} ^ {t} \boldsymbol {h} (t - \tau) \ddot {\boldsymbol {X}} (t) d \tau =
$$

$$
\boldsymbol {F} _ {\mathrm{h}} (t) + \boldsymbol {F} _ {\mathrm{d}} (t) + \boldsymbol {F} _ {\mathrm{g}} (t),\tag{2}
$$

where $A _ { \infty }$ represents the added mass matrix at infinite frehquency  and    denotes  the  acceleration  impulse-response function matrix defined by

$$
\boldsymbol {h} (t) = \frac {2}{\pi} \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} \boldsymbol {B} (\omega) \frac {\sin (\omega t)}{\omega} \mathrm{d} \omega = \frac {2}{\pi} \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} [ \boldsymbol {A} (\omega) - \boldsymbol {A} _ {\infty} ] \cos (\omega t) \mathrm{d} \omega .\tag{3}
$$

From the above equations, after calculating $\mathbf A ( \omega )$ ,  both h(t) and $A _ { \infty }$ can be determined.

(3) Regular and irregular waves with impact force

The equation of motion incorporating an impact force in both  regular  and  irregular  waves  can  be  unified  and expressed as:

$$
\begin{array}{c} (\boldsymbol {m} + \boldsymbol {A} _ {\infty}) \ddot {\boldsymbol {X}} (t) + \boldsymbol {b} \dot {\boldsymbol {X}} (t) + \boldsymbol {K X} (t) + \int_ {0} ^ {t} \boldsymbol {h} (t - \tau) \ddot {\boldsymbol {X}} (t) \mathrm{d} \tau = \\ \boldsymbol {F} _ {\mathrm{h}} (t) + \boldsymbol {F} _ {\mathrm{d}} (t) + \boldsymbol {F} _ {\mathrm{g}} (t) + \boldsymbol {F} _ {\text { impact }} (t), \end{array}\tag{4}
$$

where $F _ { \mathrm { i m p a c t } }$ represents  the  time-varying  non-periodic impact force. It should be noted that most commercial software and open-source codes utilize Eq. (1) for regular wave cases. However, since the launch impact is a transient process and non-periodic in nature, the platform’s response is inherently non-periodic; therefore, the convolution form becomes essential in calculating the radiation force, even for regular waves. Eq. (4) is solved as:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \left(\boldsymbol {m} + \boldsymbol {A} _ {\infty}\right) \ddot {\boldsymbol {X}} (t) = \boldsymbol {F} _ {\mathrm{h}} (t) + \boldsymbol {F} _ {\mathrm{d}} (t) + \boldsymbol {F} _ {\mathrm{g}} (t) + \boldsymbol {F} _ {\text { impact }} (t) - \\ \left(\boldsymbol {b} \dot {\boldsymbol {X}} (t) + \boldsymbol {K X} (t) + \int_ {0} ^ {t} \boldsymbol {h} (t - \tau) \ddot {\boldsymbol {X}} (t) \mathrm{d} \tau\right) \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

with fourth-order Runge-Kutta time-stepping scheme.

The simulation  of the  launch  process  began  with   frequency  domain  hydrodynamic  analysis  to  determine  the platform’s hydrodynamic parameters, including wave excitation  forces,  added  mass,  and  radiation  damping.  These parameters  were  subsequently  converted  into  time  domain wave  forces  corresponding  to  the  specified  wave  spectrum conditions. The software Thafts (Taihu laboratory of deepsea technological science, 2025), developed by CSSRC, was utilized  for  the  frequency  domain  hydrodynamic  analysis. For  comparative  purposes,  the  commercial  software ANSYS/Aqwa was also employed.

## 3.2.2 Validation of numerical model without impact force

The  frequency  domain  results  obtained  from  Thafts were compared with Aqwa outputs, and time domain simulations  were  conducted  using  the  in-house  code  (as  Thafts currently  lacks  time  domain  functionality)  without  impact force  for  comparison  with  Aqwa.  The  frequency  domain analysis employed 2374 nodes and 2304 meshes, determined through a convergence test. Artificial diagonal linear damping was  implemented  with  the  values  of $3 . 2 5 \times 1 0 ^ { 6 }$ N/(m/s), $2 . 8 \times 1 0 ^ { 9 }$ Nm/(rad/s), $1 . 4 4 \times 1 0 ^ { 1 0 }$ Nm/(rad/s)  for  the  heave, roll  and  pitch  motions,  respectively,  corresponding  to  3%, 5% and 8% of the critical damping.

The  Response  Amplitude  Operators  (RAOs)  of  the launch platform were calculated for various wave directions using  both  Thafts  and  Aqwa,  yielding  consistent  results. ## 4 Results and discussions

4.1  Numerical and experimental results with impact force

This study conducted two launch tests in the seakeeping basin: one in still water and another in regular waves. The numerical  results  from  the  in-house  code  simulating  the launch  process  were  compared  with  experimental  data  to validate the  numerical  method  and  examine  the   hydrodynamic characteristics of the launch platform under significant impact  force.  Impact  forces  were  measured  using  a  force sensor mounted between the rocket and platform. The force sensor  was  positioned  at  coordinates  (28.7  m,  0  m,  30  m), designated as the launch point in this study.

## 4.2  Hydrodynamic characteristic in moderate sea state

The  launch  platform  is  engineered  to  execute  launch missions  in  low  to  moderate  sea  states,  necessitating  an examination of its hydrodynamic characteristics and operational performance under these conditions.

The significant wave heights in moderate sea states are assumed  to  be  within  the  range  of 1.25 $\mathrm { m } { < } H _ { \mathrm { s } } { < } 2 . 5$ m,  the corresponding wave spectral peak periods are determined by the commonly used empirical formula below

$$
\sqrt {1 3 H _ {\mathrm{s}}} <   T _ {\mathrm{p}} <   \sqrt {3 0 H _ {\mathrm{s}}}.\tag{10}
$$

To  eliminate  uncertainties  in  the  statistical  results  of irregular  wave  simulations,  the  aforementioned  sea  states for  irregular  waves  were  transformed  into  regular  waves with equivalent  severity.  This  transformation  was   accomplished by establishing regular wave height and wave periods of $H = \sqrt { 2 } H _ { \mathrm { s m a x } } \approx 3 . 5$ m  as  standard  derivation  of  the trigonometric function $\sigma = \sqrt { 2 } / 2$ and T=8.7, 7.2, and 5.7 s to encompass the specified wave period range. The simulations considered platform responses in various wave directions, including cases without launch and with launch at different  temporal  points  relative  to  platform  motion,  as detailed in Table 3.

## 5 Conclusions

This study presents the development of an in-house code based on linear three-dimensional potential theory to simulate platform motion responses under transient impact force during sea-launch operations. The implementation of convolution integral for both regular and irregular wave simulations represents  an  innovative  approach  compared  to  existing software, particularly in handling time-varying non-periodic impact forces.  Two  launch  model  experiments  were   conducted  in  still  water  and  regular  waves.  The  numerica results from the in-house code demonstrate good agreements with  the  model  experimental  data.  Analysis  reveals  that platform motions comprise wave-induced and impact forceinduced components, with the latter being predominant during the  launching  process.  Further  investigation  of  the  selfdesigned  sea  launch  platform  in  moderate  sea  states  using the in-house code numerically demonstrates that sea launch missions  can  be  conducted  safely,  with  maximum  heave motion remaining below 0.6 m and maximum pitch motion not exceeding 1°. It should be noted that this study assumes very  high  structural  stiffness,  and  structural  deformations are not considered in the analysis.
