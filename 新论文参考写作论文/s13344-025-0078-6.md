#### Abstract

The impact force effect on launch platform motion response represents a critical safety consideration that requires thorough investigation prior to sea-launch implementation. This paper examines a self-designed semi-submersible launch platform comprising a box-shaped deck, six columns, and two pontoons, with sufficient structural stiffness to be analyzed as a rigid body. A proprietary code based on three-dimensional linear potential theory was developed for hydrodynamic analysis of the launching process. The Cummins equation was implemented to calculate platform responses under substantial impact force. The numerical results were validated through comparison with ANSYS/Aqwa commercial software for platform motion response in both launch and non-launch cases. Additionally, two model tests were conducted in a sea-keeping wave basin at a scale ratio of $\lambda=1: 40$. The numerical results demonstrated good agreement with experimental data. Both numerical and experimental findings indicate that platform motion responses result from wave-induced effects and impact force/rocket weight effects, with the latter typically predominant. Numerical simulations revealed that in moderate sea states, maximum heave and pitch motions measure 0.6 m and 1°, respectively, suggesting the viability of sea-launch operations using the designed platform under these conditions.

## 1 Introduction

Sea-based launch offers several advantages over landbased alternatives, including mobility and flexibility in launch site selection, simplified rocket transportation, and reduced risk of surrounding area damage from rocket debris, thereby garnering increasing industry attention (Song et al., 2021). Sea-based launches encompass two categories: cold launch, requiring secondary ignition after deck ejection, and thermal launch, utilizing single ignition with benefits of simplified launch systems and enhanced payload capacity per unit space (Konyukhov, 2004). However, thermal launch implementation presents challenges due to significant rocket wake impact force on the platform, potentially causing equilibrium position deviations and substantial translational and angular displacements (Wang et al., 2022). These deviations may adversely affect spacecraft attitude control during takeoff and significantly influence platform motion response (Mu et al., 2023). Therefore, quantitative analysis of platform motion under impact force is essential for determining appropriate operational conditions to ensure launch success and platform safety.

Potential theory and its derivative numerical methods are commonly employed for simulating and predicting offshore platform hydrodynamic response. Zhang et al. (2020) investigated second-order hydrodynamic effects on floating wind turbine platforms, comparing frequency and time domain analyses across three platform configurations. Pham and Shin (2020) integrated potential method with NREL FAST for fully coupled wind and wave simulations, achieving results consistent with model experiments. Zou et al. (2022, 2024) and Chen et al. (2024) employed an efficient constant parameter time-domain model (CPTDM) to examine multibody system nonlinear dynamics, demonstrating superior accuracy and efficiency particularly at resonant frequency

[^0]compared to alternative time-domain simulations. However, complex phenomena such as green water and slamming in severe sea states necessitate model experiments or CFD simulations. Rivera-Arreba et al. (2019) utilized secondorder potential-flow based solver and fully nonlinear NavierStokes equations solver to analyze semi-submersible floating platform response in non-steep waves, revealing potential flow model limitations in accurately capturing heave response and viscous effect relationships at natural frequency excitation. Paulsen et al. (2019) conducted physical model tests examining wave slamming effects on monopile wind turbine foundations, establishing probability formulas for wave slamming and associated force. Zhu et al. (2022) and Huo et al. (2023) performed model tests and CFD simulations investigating floating wind turbine slamming characteristics under freak and irregular waves respectively.

Unlike floating wind turbines or offshore drilling platforms, offshore launch platforms are subject to substantial instantaneous impact loads during rocket launches. Understanding the platform's motion response under these impact loads is essential for the overall layout design and critical for ensuring safe and stable operations. When a rocket launches from an offshore platform, the platform deviates from its equilibrium position and experiences significant translational and angular displacements. These displacements affect both spacecraft attitude control during takeoff (Cui and Hu, 2007) and the platform's motion response. Consequently, quantitative analysis of the platform's motion behavior under impact forces is necessary to determine suitable operational conditions for rocket launches and ensure platform safety. Several studies have examined the hydrodynamic response characteristics of floating platforms under impact loads from spacecraft launches. Wang et al. (2003) investigated ship motion characteristics in waves under impact force through model tests, validating their established Kane's dynamic model (Wang et al., 2001). Yu et al. (2021) analyzed the hydrodynamic response of offshore launch platforms under various conditions using ANSYS/Aqwa three-dimensional potential commercial software, examining how factors such as draft, impact load, and launch phase influence platform motion response. Pang et al. (2023) developed a test method for evaluating impact vibration acceleration response of offshore rocket launch platforms, studying launch platform impact response during cold launch through finite element analysis and shipboard experiments. Their findings revealed rapid acceleration peak decay during ejection, followed by oscillating acceleration signals during ignition. Hou (2024) employed STARCCM+ to examine motion responses of various offshore launch platform types under different wave conditions, launch positions, and platform motion phases.

Currently, limited research exists regarding spacecraft launch platform motion responses under impact loads in complex marine environments. Engineering practice requires a method for rapid prediction of launching platform hydrodynamic responses to impact loads in waves.

This paper introduces a self-designed offshore launch platform and validates its launch mission feasibility through numerical and experimental methods. Furthermore, an inhouse code based on linear three-dimensional potential theory is developed to calculate the platform's hydrodynamic response, accounting for both wave excitation and launch impact force effects. The validity and accuracy of the inhouse code are verified through comparisons with ANSYS/ Aqwa commercial software and model test results, demonstrating the method's applicability for relevant engineering applications.

This paper is arranged as follows. In Section 2, the designed launch platform is presented. In Section 3, the model test and numerical method are presented respectively, and the in-house code is verified. In Section 4, the hydrodynamic calculations of the launch platform under different sea and working conditions are carried out, and the hydrodynamic characteristics of the launch platform are analyzed in conjunction with the results of the model tests. Finally, conclusions and prospects for future work are given in Section 5.

### 3.2 Numerical method and validation

### 3.2.1 Numerical method

This study employs time domain dynamic simulations based on linear three-dimensional potential theory to investigate platform motions under substantial impact forces. Both commercial software and a self-developed code were utilized to perform numerical simulations and compare with model test results. The global earth fixed coordinate system ( $O_{0}-X_{0} Y_{0} Z_{0}$ ) originates at the mean free surface, while the local body fixed coordinate system (O-XYZ) originates at the projection of the center of gravity on the mean free surface, as defined in Fig. 4. The wave direction is designated as 0° when traveling along the $X$-axis and 90° when traveling along the $Y$-axis. The wetted surface edges of the numerical model differ slightly from the experimental model for simplification purposes, which is not expected to significantly affect platform responses. All simulations maintain zero forward speed. The numerical simulations utilize the prototype launch platform parameters.

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/f4e5bf72-fc11-4960-9b73-bc8f747efe21-04.jpg?height=450&width=792&top_left_y=1533&top_left_x=228)
Fig. 4. Wet surface of launch platform.

The equations of motion were established in the selfdeveloped in-house code for the following three cases:
(1) Regular wave without impact force

The equation of motion in the time domain can be derived directly by converting the equation in the frequency domain for regular waves:

$$
\begin{gather*}
{[\boldsymbol{m}+\boldsymbol{A}(\omega)] \ddot{\boldsymbol{X}}(t)+[\boldsymbol{b}+\boldsymbol{B}(\omega)] \dot{\boldsymbol{X}}(t)+\boldsymbol{K} \boldsymbol{X}(t)=} \\
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{h}}(t)+\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{d}}(t)+\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{g}}(t) \tag{1}
\end{gather*}
$$

where $\boldsymbol{m}$ represents the structural mass matrix, $\boldsymbol{b}$ denotes the artificial linear damping matrix, $\boldsymbol{K}$ indicates the hydrostatic stiffness matrix, $\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{h}}(t)$ represents the hydrodynamic wave incident force, $\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{d}}(t)$ denotes the wave diffraction force, and $\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{g}}(t)$ represents the gravitational force. $\boldsymbol{A}(\omega)$ and $\boldsymbol{B}(\omega)$ represent the frequency-dependent added mass and damping coefficient, respectively. $\omega$ represents the angular frequency of the wave, and $t$ denotes time.
(2) Irregular wave without impact force

The motion of the in-house code in irregular waves follows Cummins equation (Pang et al., 2023) and which is expressed in a convolution integral form:

$$
\begin{align*}
& \left(\boldsymbol{m}+\boldsymbol{A}_{\infty}\right) \ddot{\boldsymbol{X}}(t)+\boldsymbol{b} \dot{\boldsymbol{X}}(t)+\boldsymbol{K} \boldsymbol{X}(t)+\int_{0}^{t} \boldsymbol{h}(t-\tau) \ddot{\boldsymbol{X}}(t) \mathrm{d} \tau= \\
& \quad \boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{h}}(t)+\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{d}}(t)+\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{g}}(t) \tag{2}
\end{align*}
$$

where $\boldsymbol{A}_{\infty}$ represents the added mass matrix at infinite frequency and $\boldsymbol{h}$ denotes the acceleration impulse-response function matrix defined by

$$
\begin{equation*}
\boldsymbol{h}(t)=\frac{2}{\pi} \int_{0}^{\infty} \boldsymbol{B}(\omega) \frac{\sin (\omega t)}{\omega} \mathrm{d} \omega=\frac{2}{\pi} \int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\boldsymbol{A}(\omega)-\boldsymbol{A}_{\infty}\right] \cos (\omega t) \mathrm{d} \omega \tag{3}
\end{equation*}
$$

From the above equations, after calculating $\boldsymbol{A}(\omega)$, both $\boldsymbol{h}(t)$ and $\boldsymbol{A}_{\infty}$ can be determined.
(3) Regular and irregular waves with impact force

The equation of motion incorporating an impact force in both regular and irregular waves can be unified and expressed as:

$$
\begin{gather*}
\left(\boldsymbol{m}+\boldsymbol{A}_{\infty}\right) \ddot{\boldsymbol{X}}(t)+\boldsymbol{b} \dot{\boldsymbol{X}}(t)+\boldsymbol{K} \boldsymbol{X}(t)+\int_{0}^{t} \boldsymbol{h}(t-\tau) \ddot{\boldsymbol{X}}(t) \mathrm{d} \tau= \\
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{h}}(t)+\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{d}}(t)+\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{g}}(t)+\boldsymbol{F}_{\text {impact }}(t) \tag{4}
\end{gather*}
$$

where $\boldsymbol{F}_{\text {impact }}$ represents the time-varying non-periodic impact force. It should be noted that most commercial software and open-source codes utilize Eq. (1) for regular wave cases. However, since the launch impact is a transient process and non-periodic in nature, the platform's response is inherently non-periodic; therefore, the convolution form becomes essential in calculating the radiation force, even for regular waves. Eq. (4) is solved as:

$$
\begin{gather*}
\left(\boldsymbol{m}+\boldsymbol{A}_{\infty}\right) \ddot{\boldsymbol{X}}(t)=\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{h}}(t)+\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{d}}(t)+\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathrm{g}}(t)+\boldsymbol{F}_{\text {impact }}(t)- \\
\left(\boldsymbol{b} \dot{\boldsymbol{X}}(t)+\boldsymbol{K} \boldsymbol{X}(t)+\int_{0}^{t} \boldsymbol{h}(t-\tau) \ddot{\boldsymbol{X}}(t) \mathrm{d} \tau\right) \tag{5}
\end{gather*}
$$

with fourth-order Runge-Kutta time-stepping scheme.
The simulation of the launch process began with frequency domain hydrodynamic analysis to determine the platform's hydrodynamic parameters, including wave excitation forces, added mass, and radiation damping. These parameters were subsequently converted into time domain wave forces corresponding to the specified wave spectrum conditions. The software Thafts (Taihu laboratory of deepsea technological science, 2025), developed by CSSRC, was utilized for the frequency domain hydrodynamic analysis. For comparative purposes, the commercial software ANSYS/Aqwa was also employed.

### 3.2.2 Validation of numerical model without impact force

The frequency domain results obtained from Thafts were compared with Aqwa outputs, and time domain simulations were conducted using the in-house code (as Thafts currently lacks time domain functionality) without impact force for comparison with Aqwa. The frequency domain analysis employed 2374 nodes and 2304 meshes, determined through a convergence test. Artificial diagonal linear damping was implemented with the values of $3.25 \times 10^{6} \mathrm{~N} /(\mathrm{m} / \mathrm{s})$, $2.8 \times 10^{9} \mathrm{Nm} /(\mathrm{rad} / \mathrm{s}), 1.44 \times 10^{10} \mathrm{Nm} /(\mathrm{rad} / \mathrm{s})$ for the heave, roll and pitch motions, respectively, corresponding to $3 \%$, 5\% and 8\% of the critical damping.

## 5 Conclusions

This study presents the development of an in-house code based on linear three-dimensional potential theory to simulate platform motion responses under transient impact force during sea-launch operations. The implementation of convolution integral for both regular and irregular wave simulations represents an innovative approach compared to existing software, particularly in handling time-varying non-periodic impact forces. Two launch model experiments were conducted in still water and regular waves. The numerical results from the in-house code demonstrate good agreements with the model experimental data. Analysis reveals that platform motions comprise wave-induced and impact forceinduced components, with the latter being predominant during the launching process. Further investigation of the selfdesigned sea launch platform in moderate sea states using the in-house code numerically demonstrates that sea launch missions can be conducted safely, with maximum heave motion remaining below 0.6 m and maximum pitch motion not exceeding 1°. It should be noted that this study assumes very high structural stiffness, and structural deformations are not considered in the analysis.
